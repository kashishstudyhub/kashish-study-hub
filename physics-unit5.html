Electric Charge

Electric charge is a fundamental property of matter responsible for electrical interactions.

  • Positive charge
  • Negative charge

Coulomb's Law: F = (1 / 4πε₀) × (q₁q₂ / r²)

Electric Field

Electric field at a point is the force experienced per unit positive test charge placed at that point.

E = F / q

Point Charge: E = (1 / 4πε₀) × (q / r²)

Charge Distributions

Electric fields can be calculated for different continuous charge distributions using integration.

  • Line charge
  • Surface charge
  • Volume charge

dq = λ dl

dq = σ dA

dq = ρ dV

Electric Potential

Electric potential at a point is the work done per unit positive test charge in bringing it from infinity to that point.

V = W / q

Point Charge: V = (1 / 4πε₀) × (q / r)

Electric Field-Potential Relation

The electric field is related to the spatial variation of electric potential.

E = −∇V

In one dimension:

E = −dV/dx

Divergence

Divergence measures the net outward flux density of a vector field around a point.

∇ · E = ρ / ε₀

This is the differential form of Gauss's law in vacuum.

Curl

Curl describes the local rotational tendency of a vector field.

∇ × E = 0

For electrostatic fields, the curl of the electric field is zero.

Gauss's Law

The total electric flux through a closed surface equals the enclosed charge divided by ε₀.

∮ E · dA = Qenclosed / ε₀

Gauss's law is particularly useful for highly symmetric charge distributions.

Poisson's Equation

Poisson's equation relates electric potential to volume charge density.

∇²V = −ρ / ε₀

It is used to determine potential when the charge distribution is known.

Laplace's Equation

In a charge-free region, ρ = 0. Therefore, Poisson's equation reduces to Laplace's equation.

∇²V = 0

Laplace's equation is applicable in regions where there is no volume charge density.

Electrostatic Boundary Conditions

Boundary conditions specify how the electric field and potential behave at interfaces between regions.

  • Tangential component of electrostatic E is continuous.
  • Potential is continuous across an ideal interface under ordinary electrostatic conditions.

Uniqueness Theorem

The uniqueness theorem states that a solution of Poisson's or Laplace's equation is uniquely determined when appropriate boundary conditions are specified.

This theorem is important because it allows us to verify that a proposed electrostatic solution is the unique solution satisfying the given conditions.
