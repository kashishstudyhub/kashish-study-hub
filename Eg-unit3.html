1. Introduction to Projections of Planes and Solids

In engineering graphics, many objects are not represented only by points and straight lines. Objects have surfaces and volumes. Therefore, projection of planes and solids is required to represent their exact shape and position.

Plane

A plane is a flat two-dimensional surface having length and breadth but negligible thickness.

Examples of Plane Figures

  • Triangle
  • Square
  • Rectangle
  • Circle
  • Pentagon
  • Hexagon
  • Other polygons

Solid

A solid is a three-dimensional object having length, breadth and height.

Examples of Solids

  • Prism
  • Pyramid
  • Cylinder
  • Cone
  • Cube
  • Other polyhedrons

2. Reference Planes

Horizontal Plane (HP)

The horizontal plane is used for obtaining the top view of an object.

Vertical Plane (VP)

The vertical plane is used for obtaining the front view.

Profile Plane

The profile plane is used mainly for obtaining side views.

HP and VP

3. Projection of Planes

Projection of a plane means obtaining its front view and top view when the plane is placed in a particular position with respect to HP and VP.

Common Plane Figures

  • Triangle
  • Square
  • Rectangle
  • Circle
  • Pentagon
  • Hexagon

Important Positions of Plane

  1. Plane parallel to HP.
  2. Plane parallel to VP.
  3. Plane perpendicular to HP.
  4. Plane perpendicular to VP.
  5. Plane inclined to HP.
  6. Plane inclined to VP.
  7. Plane inclined to both HP and VP.

4. Plane Parallel to HP

When a plane is parallel to HP, its true shape is visible in the top view.

Characteristics

  • Top view shows the true shape.
  • Front view appears as a straight line.
  • The plane is parallel to HP.

Plane Parallel to HP

5. Plane Parallel to VP

When a plane is parallel to VP, its true shape is visible in the front view.

Characteristics

  • Front view shows true shape.
  • Top view appears as a straight line.
  • Plane is parallel to VP.

Plane Parallel to VP

6. Plane Inclined to HP

A plane may be inclined to HP at a specified angle while remaining parallel to VP.

General Method

  1. Draw the initial position of the plane.
  2. Draw its projections.
  3. Keep the required edge or surface as reference.
  4. Rotate the plane through the specified angle.
  5. Project the new position.
  6. Obtain the final top and front views.

Inclined Plane

7. Projection of a Circle

A circle is a plane figure. Its projection depends on its position with respect to HP and VP.

Position of Circle Projection
Circle parallel to HP True circle in top view
Circle parallel to VP True circle in front view
Circle inclined to HP Ellipse-like projection
Circle inclined to VP Ellipse-like projection

Circle Projection

8. Projection of Polygonal Planes

Polygonal planes such as triangles, squares, pentagons and hexagons can be projected by locating their vertices and joining the corresponding projected points.

General Procedure

  1. Draw the reference line XY.
  2. Draw the plane in its initial position.
  3. Project all vertices.
  4. Join corresponding projected points.
  5. Apply the given inclination.
  6. Project the final position.
  7. Darken the visible boundaries.

Polygonal Plane

9. Projections of Solids

A solid is a three-dimensional object. Its projection is obtained by projecting all important edges, vertices and surfaces onto HP and VP.

Classification of Solids

A. Polyhedrons

Solids bounded by plane surfaces are called polyhedrons.

  • Prism
  • Pyramid
  • Cube
  • Tetrahedron

B. Solids of Revolution

Solids generated by rotating a plane figure about an axis are called solids of revolution.

  • Cylinder
  • Cone
  • Sphere

10. Prism

Definition

A prism is a solid having two equal and parallel polygonal bases connected by rectangular lateral faces.

Types

  • Triangular prism
  • Square prism
  • Pentagonal prism
  • Hexagonal prism

Prism

11. Pyramid

Definition

A pyramid is a solid having a polygonal base and triangular faces meeting at one common point called the apex.

Examples

  • Triangular pyramid
  • Square pyramid
  • Pentagonal pyramid
  • Hexagonal pyramid

Square Pyramid

12. Cylinder

Definition

A cylinder is a solid generated by revolving a rectangle about one of its sides.

Main Parts

  • Two circular bases
  • Axis
  • Curved surface
  • Height

Cylinder

13. Cone

Definition

A cone is a solid having a circular base and a curved surface meeting at one point called the apex.

Main Parts

  • Circular base
  • Apex
  • Axis
  • Height
  • Slant height
Slant Height² = Radius² + Height²

Cone

14. Positions of Solids

For projection problems, a solid may be placed in different positions with respect to HP and VP.

Position Description
Axis perpendicular to HP Axis is normal to HP. Base appears in true shape in top view.
Axis perpendicular to VP Axis is normal to VP. Base may be represented in true shape in front view.
Axis parallel to HP Axis lies parallel to HP.
Axis parallel to VP Axis lies parallel to VP.
Axis inclined to HP Solid is tilted with respect to HP.
Axis inclined to VP Solid is tilted with respect to VP.
Axis inclined to both Solid has inclination with both reference planes.

15. Solid with Axis Perpendicular to HP

When the axis of a solid is perpendicular to HP, the base lies parallel to HP.

Result

  • Top view shows the true shape of the base.
  • Front view shows the height of the solid.
  • Axis appears perpendicular to XY in front view.

Cylinder with Axis Perpendicular to HP

16. Solid with Axis Inclined to HP

In this case the axis of the solid is inclined to HP at a given angle.

General Method

  1. Draw the solid in the simple position.
  2. Obtain its initial projections.
  3. Keep the required reference point fixed.
  4. Rotate the solid through the given angle.
  5. Project all important points.
  6. Join the corresponding points.
  7. Complete the final front and top views.

Inclined Solid

17. Auxiliary View

Definition

An auxiliary view is an additional view obtained on an auxiliary plane which is inclined to the principal reference planes.

Purpose of Auxiliary View

  • To obtain the true shape of an inclined surface.
  • To determine the true length of inclined edges.
  • To represent inclined surfaces accurately.
  • To simplify complex projection problems.

General Procedure

  1. Draw the normal front and top views.
  2. Identify the inclined surface.
  3. Draw an auxiliary reference line parallel to the surface.
  4. Project points perpendicular to the auxiliary reference line.
  5. Join the projected points.
  6. The resulting view gives the required auxiliary view.

Auxiliary Projection
